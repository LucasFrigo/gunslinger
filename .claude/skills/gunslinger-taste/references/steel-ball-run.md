# Steel Ball Run: what to borrow

*Steel Ball Run* is part 7 of Hirohiko Araki's *JoJo's Bizarre Adventure* (2004–2011): a horse race across the United States in 1890, San Diego to New York, for a $50 million purse. The user named it as a reference.

Borrow how it is built. Do not borrow what is in it: no Stands, no Spin, no Corpse Parts, no named characters, no poses traced from a cover. A concept that a reader would call "a JoJo character" has copied the surface and missed the method.

## Structural lessons

**One rule per opponent.** Every enemy has a single odd ability with a stated limit, and the fight is the hero working out the limit. Blackmore walks on raindrops, so he is only dangerous while it rains. Sugar Mountain's tree gives riches that must all be spent by sunset. For this game: give each duelist one rule the player can learn in two rounds and one tell that gives it away. Say plainly whether the rule fits the existing `AIArchetype` knobs or needs code.

**The duel as a creed.** Ringo Roadagain waits in an orchard and will only kill in a fair, declared duel, because he believes a fair duel is what makes a person whole; his power rewinds six seconds so the duel can be had properly. He is the closest thing in the book to this game. The lesson: an opponent can have a reason to stand and wait for the draw. The WAIT → DRAW ritual wants an in-world reason, and different duelists can hold it for different reasons (honour, superstition, a contract, a bet).

**Motive is small and concrete.** Gyro Zeppeli is a state executioner from Naples who enters the race to win an amnesty for one condemned boy. Not revenge, not destiny. Give each character a want that fits in one sentence and involves a specific person, debt, or object.

**The body carries the story.** Johnny Joestar is a star jockey who was shot and cannot walk; Gyro has "GO! GO! ZEPPELI" engraved across his teeth; Wekapipo's attack takes away the left half of what you can perceive. Injury, prosthesis, and ornament are biography you can see at range. Half-head already works this way.

**Menace and comedy in the same person.** Gyro tells terrible jokes and carries a teddy bear. The president of the United States is the villain and is sincere about it. The tone is grotesque, funny, and earnest at once, never grimdark and never parody.

**History bent one notch.** Real year, real route, real institutions, one impossible thing added. The 23rd president is invented but the office is real. For lore, start from a real place, trade, or event of the 1870s–1890s and change one fact.

**Stages have personalities.** Each leg of the race has terrain that decides who can win it: desert, mountains, river crossings, a frozen strait. Arenas and gauntlet rungs can carry character the same way; the place is half the opponent.

**The horse is cast.** Slow Dancer, Valkyrie, and Silver Bullet are named, drawn distinctly, and matter to the plot. If a character rides, the mount has a name and a look.

**Names from elsewhere.** Araki lifts names from songs, bands, and fashion labels (Hot Pants, Mountain Tim, Funny Valentine, Ringo Roadagain). They sound wrong for 1890 and are impossible to forget. Names here can come from Brazilian and American music, brand names, dance steps, card games, cattle brands.

**No fixed palette.** Araki recolours the same character on every cover: green horses, pink skies, a blue-lipped hero. Colour is chosen for the image. A character's signature is shape and pattern; the hue can be anything loud.

## Visual habits worth studying

- Hats as engineering: slotted brims, goggles built in, a cap printed with stars.
- Clothing covered in a single repeated motif (stars, horseshoes, studs, hearts).
- Bodies twisted past comfort; a standing figure is never simply standing.
- Covers composed like fashion plates, with art nouveau lettering.
